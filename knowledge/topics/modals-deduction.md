# Topic: Modals of Deduction

## Objective
Teach the student to express certainty and probability with must, might, may, could, and can't.

## Teaching Strategy

### 1. Deduction About the Present
- "She must be tired." = I am almost sure.
- "He might be at home." = possible, but not certain.
- "They can't be serious." = I believe this is impossible.
- Ask: "Are you certain, unsure, or sure something is impossible?"

### 2. Deduction About the Past
- Use "must have," "might have," and "can't have" + past participle.
- "She must have forgotten."
- "He might have missed the train."
- "They can't have seen us."
- Ask: "If you are guessing about a finished past event, what do you add after the modal?"

### 3. Meaning, Not Obligation
- Here, "must" is not obligation. It is strong logical belief.
- Compare: "You must go" (obligation) vs. "You must be tired" (deduction).
- Keep the student focused on the speaker's degree of certainty.

## Block-Building Example
- "She" + "must be" + "busy"
- "He" + "might have" + "forgotten"
- Combine: "She must be busy." / "He might have forgotten."

## Common Pitfalls
- Using "mustn't" when the meaning is deduction. For deduction, "can't" is usually the natural negative.
- Forgetting the bare infinitive after the modal in present deduction ("must be," not "must is").
- Forgetting the past participle in past deduction ("might have forgot" instead of "might have forgotten").
- Guide the student to discover the meaning scale: "How sure are you?"

## Connection to Other Concepts
- Builds on modals-can-should.
- Reuses past participles from present-perfect-simple and past-perfect.
