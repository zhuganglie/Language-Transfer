# Topic: Stylistic Flexibility

## Objective
Teach the student to adjust English based on audience, context, and intention rather than speaking in one fixed style.

## Teaching Strategy

### 1. Formal vs Informal Choices
- English often has two ways to say the same thing.
- "Put off" vs "postpone," "get in touch" vs "contact," "kids" vs "children."
- Ask: "If you are writing to a university, would you choose 'kids' or 'children'?"

### 2. Directness and Softening
- Style is not only vocabulary. It is also how strongly or softly you speak.
- "Send me the file" is direct. "Could you send me the file?" is softer.
- Ask: "How does the tone change when we add 'could you'?"

### 3. Matching the Situation
- A good speaker can move between conversation, email, presentation, and essay.
- The goal is control, not permanent formality.
- Ask: "If you spoke to a friend and wrote a job application in exactly the same voice, would that feel natural?"

## Block-Building Example
- Informal: "I want to talk about the problem."
- More formal: "I would like" + "to discuss" + "the issue."
- Softer request: "Could you" + "send me" + "the report?"

## Common Pitfalls
- Sounding too formal in casual conversation.
- Sounding too casual in professional writing.
- Thinking "advanced" always means "more formal."
- Guide the student to hear the audience: "Who are you speaking to, and what relationship do you have with them?"

## Connection to Other Concepts
- Draws on advanced-connectors, idiomatic-nuance, and regional-variation.
- This is one of the final steps toward sounding intentional rather than merely correct.
